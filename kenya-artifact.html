everything follows from one number

Total dynamic head

The pump has to lift water from the drawdown level in the borehole, up a 9 m tower, against friction in 150 m of rising main. Add those and you get the number every other decision hangs off:

58 m drawdown  +  9 m tower  +  3 m friction  =  70 m TDH

Delivery pressure comes free after that. The tank bottom sits 9 m up, a waist-high tap is 1 m off the ground, so 8 m of head reaches the tap:

ρgh = 1000 × 9.81 × 8 = 78,480 Pa = 11.4 psi

Against a WASH minimum of 5 psi, every tap from ground level to 2 m passes with margin. No booster pump, no second failure point.

the rated point is not the operating point

Pump sizing

The quoted pump was a 4 kW seven-stage submersible, referenced at its rated point: 15 m³/hr at 62 m of head. That reading was flagged in review as too low to design against — and it is, because 62 m is not where this pump will actually sit.

Our system curve meets it at 70 m. Interpolating between the manufacturer’s 6 m³/hr and 12 m³/hr points puts the real operating flow near 11 m³/hr — lower than rated, and still comfortably enough:

  • 22,000 L of daily demand in 2.0 hours of pumping.
  • Both tanks filled from empty in 1.8 hours.
  • Against an 8-hour solar window.

If the aquifer draws down past 58 m, TDH climbs and flow falls off the same curve. Worth monitoring the water level after installation rather than assuming today’s number holds.

Filter media

Two candidates for defluoridation: hydroxylapatite (HAP Aqua) and activated alumina. Alumina is cheaper per kilogram, which is the argument for it, and the argument stops there.

Activated alumina can leach aluminium at high pH. On an industrial line that is a monitoring problem; on a secondary school’s only drinking water it is a reason to walk away. We stayed with HAP: higher adsorption capacity, effectively no ion leakage, food-grade regeneration chemicals, and a supplier already engaged locally who had quoted the barrels.

Then the check that decides whether gravity feed is even possible. Carman-Kozeny through the packed bed at the required 5.5 m/h face velocity gives 0.37 m of head loss, against 9 m available from the tower. Gravity feed works with room to spare — enough that the inlet needs a throttling valve to stop the filter running over-velocity.

Solar

Fifteen 600 W modules, 9 kW total, into a 4 kW three-phase inverter driving the pump motor directly. The inverter manufacturer wants the array at 1.3× motor size or better — 5.2 kW minimum. At 9 kW we are 73% above that.

At the equator and 2,400 m the site sees roughly 5 peak sun hours. Harvest lands near 38 kWh/day against the 8 kWh the pump actually needs.

That surplus is the design, not an accident. Oversizing is what buys you cloudy days, morning and evening shoulders when the array is nowhere near peak, and room for the school to grow into the system.

Storage

Phase 1 puts 20,000 L on site: two 5,000 L ground tanks for raw water and one 10,000 L elevated tank for filtered. Demand is 22,000 L/day. That is 0.9 days of storage, and the standard people usually reach for is two days.

It works because the pump refills everything in under two hours, so the tanks turn over daily. What it does not have is a buffer for a genuinely dark day. We documented that as a Phase 1 constraint with a number attached rather than letting it be discovered later — and the expansion plan adds a 20,000 L tank at Phase 2.

Even the plan runs short: at Phase 4 population, 60,000 L is below a two-day buffer. That gets reassessed at the Phase 3 review rather than assumed away now.
